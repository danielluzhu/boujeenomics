The definitions

What these words mean here

Starter home

A home in the bottom third of its market by value — specifically Zillow's bottom-tier home value index, the typical home between the 5th and 35th percentile. It is whatever a first-time buyer can actually reach in that city, not a fixed size. Nationally it is usually a 2–3 bedroom house of around 1,000–1,400 sq ft. In San Francisco, New York and Los Angeles it is often a one- or two-bedroom condo or co-op.

Minimum down payment

House hack

The buyer lives in one bedroom of a three-bedroom starter home and rents out the other two. Each room is assumed to rent for of what the whole home would, less vacancy. The loan, the down payment and the costs are the same as living in it alone. In the cities where the starter tier is mostly small condos, a three-bedroom at that price is hard to find, so read the house hack there as a best case.

Rent it out

An investor buys a home and lets the whole thing. A loan for a home you won't live in needs down and costs about points more in interest. The investor buys whichever home type had the best cap rate at the moment of purchase, and pays of collected rent for management.

The comparison

Every strategy starts with the same money: the down payment. After that the budgets are matched year by year. If owning costs more than renting, the renter invests the difference in the S&P 500 or in gold. If owning costs less, the owner invests the difference in the S&P 500. The investor is compared against investing the down payment, and any later cash the rental needs, in stocks or gold instead. So each line is the same person spending the same money.

After sale

Home values are shown lower than the market value, which is the typical cost of selling (agent and closing). Without that deduction, owning would look better than it is, because a home's value can't be spent until it's sold.
